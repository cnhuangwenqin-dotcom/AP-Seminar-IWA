# Rubric Calibration for AP Seminar IWA Review

Use this reference to imitate College Board-style scoring habits while giving detailed coaching. This is a paraphrased operational guide, not a replacement for the official rubric.

## Global scoring habits

- Score the seven rows independently.
- Use the allowed point values only: 5/5/9/12/9/5/3 for a 48-point maximum.
- Apply a preponderance-of-evidence standard. Do not require perfection for high scores, but do not reward isolated strong moments if the row requirement is not sustained.
- Be student-oriented: if a current-year stimulus-derived theme is inferable from at least two stimulus sources, the paper is normally scoreable. If a possible off-topic issue exists, flag it as a risk rather than confidently declaring all-zero unless the user asks for a hard audit and the evidence is unmistakable.
- A paper that cites only previous-year stimulus material and no current-year stimulus material is a serious off-topic risk.
- Missing bibliography or mostly missing in-text citations is a Row 6 problem and can also reduce clarity in Rows 3-5 because attribution becomes unclear.

## Row 1: stimulus integration (0 or 5)

Question: Does at least one stimulus material contribute authentically to the argument?

Award 5 when:
- The student accurately understands at least one stimulus source's context and meaning.
- The stimulus performs a real argumentative function, such as motivating the research question, establishing context, or supporting a relevant claim.
- Removing the stimulus reference would weaken the argument.

Score 0 when:
- No stimulus material is used.
- The stimulus is only mentioned in a sentence, used as a decorative hook, used only for a generic definition, misrepresented, or could be deleted with little effect.

Coaching standard:
- Recommend one or two stimulus sources, not a pile of them.
- Strong drafts often introduce a stimulus source early to justify the research question and then return to the source as evidence or conceptual framing.

## Row 2: context and significance (0 or 5)

Question: Does the paper situate the research question in a specific, important larger context?

Award 5 when:
- The topic is narrow enough to sustain complexity.
- The introduction or early section clarifies who/what/where/when.
- The paper explains why the question matters with concrete, relevant details, statistics, urgency, stakes, or consequences.
- The established context remains relevant throughout the argument.

Score 0 when:
- The context is generic, broad, or only says the topic is important.
- The draft gives context for only part of the question.
- The context is tangential, unsupported, or overly scattered.

## Row 3: perspectives (0, 6, or 9)

Question: Does the paper evaluate multiple source-based arguments and put them in dialogue?

Score 9 when:
- Multiple perspectives are clearly attributed to sources.
- The student evaluates relationships among them, such as agreement, tension, contradiction, qualification, limitation, implication, or relative strength.
- The draft uses the details of the sources' arguments, not just their topics.
- Objections or limitations are considered in a way that shapes the student's conclusion.

Score 6 when:
- Multiple perspectives are present and somewhat compared, but the comparison is basic.
- The draft says sources agree/disagree without explaining how or why in a nuanced way.
- Some perspectives are source-based, but others drift into stakeholder labels or unsupported opinion.

Score 0 when:
- The paper has only one perspective.
- Perspectives are personal opinions, topic lenses, facts, or stakeholder labels with no source argument.
- Perspectives are isolated summaries with no comparison.

Important distinction:
- A perspective is a point of view conveyed through an argument. A lens, topic, stakeholder group, or fact is not automatically a perspective.

## Row 4: argument and line of reasoning (0, 8, or 12)

Question: Does the paper make a clear, convincing argument driven by student reasoning?

Score 12 when:
- The argument is logically organized and often explicitly signposted.
- Claims build toward one controlling answer to the research question.
- Evidence is followed by commentary that explains why it supports the claim and how it advances the argument.
- Student voice controls the paper; sources contribute to the argument rather than drive the paper.
- Counterclaims or alternate views create nuance.
- The conclusion/resolution/solution is plausible, specific, and aligned to the research question, with limitations or implications when relevant.

Score 8 when:
- The paper either organizes well or explains evidence well, but not both.
- Commentary is present but underdeveloped or repetitive.
- Evidence often drives the paper, creating a report-like feel.
- Claims lack cohesion or create several loosely related mini-arguments.
- The conclusion only partly answers the research question or lacks enough specificity to assess plausibility.

Score 0 when:
- The paper is mostly summary, report, chronicle, or unsupported opinion.
- The argument is difficult to discern, contradictory, or lacks a conclusion/resolution.
- Claims do not allow for alternative views.

## Row 5: evidence selection and use (0, 6, or 9)

Question: Is the evidence relevant, credible, sufficient, and purposefully used?

Score 9 when:
- Evidence is relevant to the topic and directly supports claims.
- Sources are appropriate for an academic argument, including scholarly or well-vetted work.
- The paper establishes source credibility through attribution, context, or effective citation.
- Evidence is analyzed and evaluated, not simply inserted.
- The source base is sufficient for the scope of the research question.

Score 6 when:
- Sources are mostly relevant and credible, but the academic source base is thin, inconsistent, overly journalistic, or sometimes merely referenced.
- Credibility is established inconsistently.
- Some evidence supports claims only generally or with gaps.
- Scholarly work may exist but be used for broad/simple points.

Score 0 when:
- Evidence is mostly irrelevant or lacks credibility.
- There is no well-vetted source beyond stimulus material.
- A well-vetted source is present but trivial, misrepresented, or not aligned to a claim.

Coaching standard:
- For many high-scoring IWA drafts, 10-15 total sources is a useful target, but source quality and use matter more than raw count.
- General encyclopedias and dictionaries do not count as well-vetted academic evidence.

## Row 6: citation and attribution conventions (0, 3, or 5)

Question: Are sources attributed, cited, and listed consistently enough for a reader to track them?

Score 5 when:
- In-text citations/footnotes and bibliography entries are consistently linked.
- The bibliography has a clear organizational principle, usually alphabetical or numerical.
- Essential elements are present for most sources: author/organization, title, publication/container, date, and location such as URL/DOI when appropriate.
- Paraphrased material has clear attribution and/or parenthetical citation.
- Flaws are few and minor.

Score 3 when:
- A style is generally consistent but has repeated errors or missing elements.
- Some in-text citations do not clearly match references, but many do.
- Attribution exists but is inconsistent or unclear.

Score 0 when:
- Bibliography is missing, or in-text citations are largely missing.
- Only one side exists: in-text citations without bibliography, or bibliography without in-text citations.
- The bibliography has no clear organizational principle.
- In-text citation labels do not match bibliography labels enough for a reader to link them.
- Paraphrases rely on vague tags like "research says" without citation.

## Row 7: academic writing style (0, 2, or 3)

Question: Is the student's own prose clear, controlled, and appropriate for an academic audience?

Score 3 when:
- Prose is clear and precise enough to communicate complex ideas.
- Sentence structure has variety and control.
- Tone is consistently academic with only minor errors.

Score 2 when:
- Meaning is mostly clear but there are awkward constructions, imprecise words, run-ons/fragments, dense prose, or occasional colloquial language.

Score 0 when:
- Grammar/style problems make the paper hard to understand.
- The style is broadly inappropriate for academic writing.
- Student writing is too sparse or indistinguishable from source paraphrase.
